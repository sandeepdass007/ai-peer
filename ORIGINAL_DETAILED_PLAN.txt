AI Peer: Build Your Own Local AI Teacher — From Ground Zero to Hero
Your idea is excellent, and I would approach it as a serious AI engineering project rather than simply building a chatbot with a document-upload feature.

What you are describing is a personal, locally running AI learning companion that understands your learning goals, teaches at your pace, remembers what you have learned, identifies gaps in your understanding, and uses different AI agents and tools to help you master a subject.

Imagine studying a topic such as LangGraph.

Instead of giving you a 5,000-word explanation, AI Peer would:

Assess your current understanding of Python, LLMs, and AI agents.
Start with the prerequisites you need.
Explain one concept at a time using simple language.
Generate diagrams to help you visualize the concept.
Give you a small coding exercise.
Evaluate your answer and identify misunderstandings.
Explain the concept differently if you are struggling.
Increase the difficulty when you demonstrate understanding.
Create notes, flashcards, quizzes, and presentations from what you learn.
Remember your progress when you return the next day.
And all of this should work on your local PC, with your learning materials available as its knowledge base.

That is the AI Peer I would build.

I'll explain the architecture, technology stack, agents, memory systems, learning engine, local models, development roadmap, and the decisions you should make before writing code.

1. First, Define What AI Peer Actually Is
I would divide AI Peer into seven major systems.

System	Responsibility
AI Tutor	Teaches concepts, asks questions, and adapts explanations.
Knowledge Engine	Ingests books, PDFs, PPTs, transcripts, and other learning materials.
Learning Engine	Tracks mastery, identifies knowledge gaps, and manages learning progression.
Agent System	Coordinates specialized agents for research, diagrams, assessment, and content generation.
Memory System	Remembers learning history, preferences, mistakes, and progress.
Content Studio	Generates notes, PDFs, ebooks, presentations, diagrams, and revision material.
Local Runtime	Runs the models, databases, application, and processing pipeline on your PC.
These systems should work together, but they should not all be implemented simultaneously.

The most important architectural principle is:

AI Peer should be a learning management system powered by AI, not merely an AI chatbot connected to documents.

A chatbot can retrieve a paragraph from a book and explain it. A genuine AI tutor must also decide what to teach next, whether you understand the current concept, and when you are ready to progress.

That distinction should drive your entire design.

2. The Overall Technical Architecture
Before selecting individual libraries, let's establish how the components will communicate.

Here is the architecture I recommend.

Your application will have five main technical layers.

Layer 1: User Interface
This is where you interact with AI Peer.

Which agent or workflow should execute?
Should the response include a diagram, code, or quiz?
Layer 3: AI Orchestration
This is where LangGraph coordinates the teaching process and specialized agents.

It controls the sequence of operations and manages conditional decisions.

For example:

User question → Assess understanding → Retrieve knowledge → Teach → Ask question → Evaluate answer → Decide next step

If you understand the concept, the workflow advances.

If you struggle, the workflow returns to the explanation stage.

Layer 4: Knowledge and Memory
This layer contains your learning materials and information about your learning journey.

It can include:

Original documents.
Extracted text.

Knowledge graphs.

Learning history.

Layer 5: Local AI Runtime
This layer runs the language models and embedding models.

It should allow AI Peer to operate without depending on a cloud LLM API.

4. The Knowledge Engine: RAG, Vector Databases, and Knowledge Graphs
This is one of the most important parts of your project.

You want AI Peer to learn from YouTube videos, transcripts, books, PDFs, PowerPoint presentations, and other educational materials.

The challenge is not merely storing those files.

The challenge is making their contents searchable, trustworthy, understandable, and useful for teaching.

I would build a knowledge engine with three complementary retrieval mechanisms.

A. RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation
RAG allows the model to retrieve relevant information from your own learning materials before generating an answer.

Suppose you upload a book about neural networks.

Without RAG, your local model relies primarily on the knowledge encoded in its model parameters and whatever information is supplied in the current prompt.

With RAG, AI Peer can retrieve relevant passages from your book and use them when explaining a concept.

The basic pipeline is:

PDF / PPT / Transcript / Book
             |
             v
      Extract Content
             |
             v
     Clean and Normalize
             |
             v
    Split into Meaningful Chunks
             |
             v
       Generate Embeddings
             |
             v
      Store in Vector Database
             |
             v
       User Asks a Question
             |
             v
      Retrieve Relevant Chunks
             |
             v
       Rerank the Results
             |
             v
    LLM Generates Grounded Answer
             |
             v
      Display Answer + Sources
Recommended document-processing libraries
Task	Suggested technology
PDF text extraction	PyMuPDF
PowerPoint extraction	python-pptx
Word document extraction	python-docx
EPUB processing	ebooklib
General document loading and splitting	LangChain integrations or custom loaders
Structured document validation	Pydantic
For complex books and scanned documents, you may need OCR, layout-aware extraction, or specialized parsing.

A scanned PDF containing images of pages cannot always be processed adequately using ordinary text extraction.

How should documents be chunked?
Do not split everything into arbitrary blocks of 500 characters.

Instead, use document structure whenever possible.

For example, a book might contain:

Chapter 3: Neural Networks
    |
    +-- 3.1 Biological Inspiration
    |
    +-- 3.2 Artificial Neurons
    |
    +-- 3.3 Activation Functions
    |
    +-- 3.4 Forward Propagation
    |
    +-- 3.5 Backpropagation
Preserve metadata for each chunk:

{
  "document_id": "neural_network_book",
  "document_title": "Introduction to Neural Networks",
  "chapter": "Forward Propagation",
  "page_start": 42,
  "page_end": 44,
  "chunk_id": "chunk_0042",
  "content": "..."
}

The metadata is as important as the text.

It enables AI Peer to cite sources, return to the relevant page, group content by chapter, and distinguish one source from another.

My recommendation for retrieval
Start with hybrid retrieval rather than relying entirely on vector similarity.

Combine:

Vector search: Finds semantically related passages.
Keyword search: Finds exact terms, acronyms, identifiers, and technical phrases.
Metadata filtering: Restricts results to a particular course, book, chapter, or lesson.
Reranking: Reorders candidate passages by their relevance to the question.
For example, vector search may identify the general topic of gradient descent, while keyword search ensures that a passage explicitly discussing the term learning_rate is not overlooked.

For the initial implementation, do not overcomplicate the pipeline. Establish reliable document extraction, chunking, citations, and retrieval first.

B. Vector database: Which one should you use?
I would begin with Qdrant or Chroma.

Official websites:

Qdrant
Chroma
Both can support a local retrieval system.

Requirement	Recommendation
Simple local prototype	Chroma
More retrieval controls and a service-oriented deployment path	Qdrant
Existing SQL-centric application with modest vector requirements	Evaluate PostgreSQL with pgvector
Choose one initially.

You do not need all three.

The vector database stores embeddings and associated metadata. It does not replace your original files or your relational database.

Keep the original documents so you can reprocess them when your chunking or embedding strategy changes.

That is often a better engineering decision than deploying multiple databases before you know which graph queries you need.

5. The Hybrid Knowledge Retrieval System
I would not treat vector search and graph search as competing technologies.

Use them together.

Suppose you ask:

Teach me backpropagation, assuming I understand basic neural networks but have never studied calculus.

AI Peer could execute the following process.

Step 1: Understand the learning request

Identify the topic, learning objective, and stated prerequisite knowledge.

Step 2: Query the learner profile

Retrieve the concepts you have already mastered and the concepts you have not yet studied.

Step 3: Query the knowledge graph

Identify prerequisites for backpropagation.

For example:

Functions and variables.
Basic derivatives.
The chain rule.
Forward propagation.
Loss functions.
Step 4: Query the vector database

Retrieve relevant explanations from your books, transcripts, and notes.

Step 5: Combine the results

Construct a lesson plan based on the prerequisites you need and the resources you supplied.

Step 6: Teach the lesson

Explain the concept in manageable steps and cite the relevant sources.

Step 7: Assess understanding

Ask a question that tests the actual concept rather than merely asking whether the explanation made sense.

Step 8: Update learning progress

If you struggle with the chain rule, revisit it before continuing.

The resulting system combines semantic retrieval, graph traversal, learner state, and controlled teaching workflows.

I would call this Graph-enhanced RAG with learner-aware orchestration.

It is a good architectural direction for AI Peer, but you should implement it incrementally.

6. The Most Important Component: The Learning Engine
This is where I would invest the greatest amount of design effort.

Your goal is not simply to explain everything from beginner to advanced.

Your goal is to help the learner understand and retain the material.

That requires a model of the learner.

A. Build a learner profile
For each learner, maintain a structured profile.

For example:

{
  "learner_id": "local_user",
  "course_id": "deep_learning",
  "current_level": "beginner",
  "preferred_explanation_style": "visual",
  "learning_goal": "understand neural networks",
  "mastered_concepts": [
    "basic_python",
    "variables",
    "functions"
  ],
  "concepts_to_revisit": [
    "derivatives"
  ],
  "current_lesson": "activation_functions",
  "preferred_pace": "one_concept_at_a_time"
}

This is illustrative data, not a schema you must adopt exactly.

The profile should be editable by the learner.

Do not infer permanent preferences from one interaction or treat the model's judgments as unquestionable facts.

B. Use explicit concept mastery
A topic's completion status should not be based solely on whether you read its explanation.

Instead, track evidence of understanding.

For each concept, record:

Whether it has been introduced.
Whether the learner can explain it.
Whether the learner can apply it.
Whether the learner can distinguish it from related concepts.
Whether the learner can recall it later.
When it should be reviewed.
A simple mastery model might use:

Score	Interpretation	Next action
0	Not introduced	Teach prerequisites
1	Introduced, little evidence of understanding	Explain and demonstrate
2	Partial understanding	Give guided practice
3	Demonstrated basic understanding	Try independent practice
4	Strong understanding	Move forward and schedule revision
These scores are a proposed starting framework, not a scientifically validated universal scale.

The system should store evidence supporting a mastery judgment.

For example:

Concept: Gradient Descent

Evidence:
- Correctly explained the purpose of the learning rate.
- Solved one guided numerical example.
- Made an error when interpreting a negative gradient.

Estimated mastery: Developing

Recommended next step:
- Practice interpreting the gradient direction.
This is far more useful than storing a simple completed: true flag.

You specifically mentioned that AI Peer should proceed at the user's pace rather than rushing through material.

I would implement that requirement explicitly.

The model can help interpret free-form answers, but important transitions should be governed by explicit rules.

For example, the system should not automatically advance because the LLM says the student appears confident.

Instead, evaluate the answer against the intended learning objective.

What if the learner says, "I still don't understand"?
AI Peer should not repeat the same explanation with slightly different wording.

It should select a different teaching strategy.

For example:

Use a real-world analogy.
Provide a visual explanation.
Work through a numerical example.
Show a minimal code example.
Ask a simpler diagnostic question.
Identify the missing prerequisite.
This is one of the most valuable features you can build.

D. Implement spaced repetition
Learning should continue after the initial lesson.

Suppose you understand gradient descent today but forget the details next week.

AI Peer should schedule a short review.

You could implement a spaced-repetition algorithm or integrate a suitable scheduling library.

A practical first version might schedule reviews based on:

Correctness.
Response confidence.
Time since the last review.
Number of successful retrieval attempts.
Importance of the prerequisite.
Do not overengineer this initially. A straightforward review schedule is sufficient to validate the feature.

The learning engine should control what comes next. The LLM should help deliver the lesson, not independently control the entire educational process.

7. The Multi-Agent System
Now let's discuss the agents you mentioned.

I would avoid creating a dozen autonomous agents that can call one another without clear constraints.

Instead, create a small number of specialized capabilities, orchestrated by LangGraph.

The agents should have clear responsibilities and defined inputs and outputs.

Recommended AI Peer agents
Agent	Responsibility	Example output
1. Tutor Agent	Explain concepts and answer questions	Step-by-step lesson
2. Curriculum Agent	Build and adjust the learning sequence	Prerequisite-aware lesson plan
3. Research Agent	Retrieve and synthesize source material	Evidence-backed explanation
4. Assessment Agent	Generate and evaluate exercises	Quiz, feedback, mastery evidence
5. Diagram Agent	Generate visual representations	Flowcharts, concept maps, diagrams
6. Coding Agent	Explain code and create programming exercises	Examples and runnable exercises
7. Content Agent	Generate reusable learning materials	Notes, PDFs, ebooks, presentations
8. Review Agent	Identify gaps and schedule revision	Review plan and flashcards
These do not all need to be separate LLM-powered agents.

Some are better implemented as deterministic tools or structured workflows.

For example, exporting a presentation is a tool-driven process. It does not need an autonomous agent deciding how to write files.

Agent architecture
                    LangGraph
                        |

                        |

          |             |             |

In practice, some agents may execute sequentially, while others may run in parallel when their tasks are independent.

For example, after retrieving the relevant material, AI Peer could generate a text explanation and a draft diagram specification in parallel.

However, assessment and curriculum decisions should respect the learner's current state.

Should every agent use a separate model?
No.

Start with one local LLM and different system prompts, structured output schemas, and tools.

Consider separate models only after measuring a real benefit.

Possible reasons include:

A smaller model is sufficiently accurate for document classification.
A stronger model is needed for difficult explanations.
A specialized vision model is needed for image understanding.
A code-focused model performs better on programming tasks.
More models introduce additional memory requirements and operational complexity.

9. Ingesting YouTube Videos, Books, PDFs, and PowerPoint Files
AI Peer needs a robust ingestion pipeline.

I would design one shared interface for all supported sources.

A. YouTube videos
There are two primary approaches.

Option 1: Use existing transcripts.

If a suitable transcript is available, ingest it directly.

Possible tools include YouTube transcript extraction libraries and the official YouTube Data API where its supported capabilities and access conditions fit your needs.

Option 2: Transcribe the audio locally.

If a transcript is unavailable and you have the right to process the content, extract the audio and use a local speech-recognition model such as Whisper.

11. Memory: What Should AI Peer Remember?
Memory is not one single database.

I would separate it into four categories.

A. Conversation memory
Stores recent dialogue relevant to the current lesson.

Example:

We have already established that a gradient describes the direction of steepest increase locally.

This helps prevent unnecessary repetition within a conversation.

B. Episodic learning memory
Stores significant events from previous learning sessions.

Examples:

The learner struggled with the chain rule.
A visual explanation was successful.
A specific exercise was completed.
The learner requested more numerical examples.
This can be stored as structured records rather than relying entirely on free-form summaries.

C. Semantic knowledge memory
Stores information about concepts and their relationships.

Examples:

Backpropagation depends on the chain rule.
Gradient descent uses gradient information to update parameters.
A particular chapter explains activation functions.
This belongs in the knowledge layer.

D. Learner progress memory
Stores the current learning state.

Examples:

Current course.
Current module.
Estimated mastery.
Completed exercises.
Scheduled revisions.
Learning objectives.
This should be stored as structured, queryable data.

Should you use LangGraph persistence?
Yes.

LangGraph supports checkpointing workflow state so an interrupted process can resume.

That is useful when a learner stops halfway through a lesson.

However, workflow checkpoints do not replace a proper learner database.

For example:

A checkpoint remembers where a workflow stopped.
A learning record remembers whether the student demonstrated mastery.
A knowledge graph represents prerequisite relationships.
These are related but distinct responsibilities.

For AI Peer, use explicit learner records as the authoritative source of learning progress.

16. How to Make the Tutor Actually Teach Well
The quality of AI Peer will depend on more than its underlying model.

You need a consistent teaching protocol.

I would define the following teaching contract.

Whenever AI Peer introduces a new concept, it should normally provide:

Learning objective: What the learner will understand.
Prerequisite check: What the learner needs to know first.
Intuition: A simple explanation of the underlying idea.
Formal explanation: The technical definition or mechanism.
Example: A concrete demonstration.
Visualization: A diagram where appropriate.
Misconceptions: Common mistakes and how to avoid them.
Understanding check: A question that tests the concept.
Feedback: An explanation of why an answer is correct or incorrect.
Next step: Continue, practice, or revisit a prerequisite.
Not every response needs all ten elements. Doing so would make conversations unnecessarily long.

Instead, treat this as a teaching framework and select the appropriate elements for the learner's current needs.

17. How to Evaluate Whether AI Peer Is Working
Do not judge the application only by whether its answers sound intelligent.

You need tests for the entire learning experience.

I would create four evaluation suites.

A. Knowledge retrieval evaluation
Create questions whose answers are known to exist in your source materials.

Measure:

Whether the correct passage is retrieved.
Whether relevant pages or timestamps are cited.
Whether the system retrieves contradictory information.
Whether it acknowledges missing evidence.
B. Teaching quality evaluation
Ask learners or evaluators to rate:

Clarity.
Correctness.
Appropriateness for the learner's level.
Quality of examples.
Quality of diagrams.
Usefulness of feedback.
C. Assessment evaluation
Check whether:

Questions match the learning objectives.
Answer keys are correct.
Rubrics are clear.
Equivalent valid answers are accepted.
Incorrect answers receive useful feedback.
Mastery decisions are consistent.
D. Learning progression evaluation
Test whether the system:

Identifies missing prerequisites.
Avoids advancing too early.
Avoids unnecessary repetition.
Resumes interrupted lessons.
Schedules useful reviews.
Remembers prior mistakes.
Build a small test dataset

Include:

Simple factual questions.
Conceptual questions.
Questions requiring multiple source passages.
Questions whose answers are not in the supplied documents.
Questions that test prerequisite relationships.
Questions designed to expose common misconceptions.
Use the same tests whenever you change models, prompts, chunking strategies, or retrieval logic.

This is how you will know whether an architectural change actually improves AI Peer.

19. What I Would NOT Build First
This is important because your idea can expand almost indefinitely.

I would postpone the following:

Too many agents create coordination problems and make failures harder to debug.

2. A dedicated graph database
First, prove that prerequisite traversal and concept relationships improve the teaching experience.

A simple graph representation may be enough initially.

3. Automatic ingestion of every type of document
Start with PDF, PPTX, and plain-text transcripts.

Add EPUB, scanned books, audio, video frames, and specialized formats incrementally.

4. Local image generation
First implement accurate, structured diagrams.

Then add illustrative image generation if your hardware supports it and you have a useful application for it.

You probably do not need fine-tuning to get started.

Improve the application through:

Better retrieval.
Clear teaching instructions.
Explicit learner state.
Structured assessment.
Evaluation datasets.
Better workflow design.
Consider fine-tuning only when you have a well-defined performance problem that cannot be adequately solved through those methods.

Use a simple interface until you know how the teaching process should work.

If AI Peer eventually runs code for exercises, isolate execution in a restricted environment.

20. Important Design Risks and How to Address Them
A strong architecture also needs to anticipate failure modes.

Risk	Mitigation
The model invents information	Ground explanations in retrieved evidence and test factual claims.
Retrieval misses the right passage	Combine semantic search, keyword search, metadata filtering, and reranking.
The tutor advances too quickly	Use explicit mastery rules and prerequisite checks.
The tutor repeats itself	Track prior explanations and choose a different teaching strategy.
Knowledge graphs contain incorrect relationships	Preserve provenance and validate extracted relationships before using them in curriculum decisions.
Uploaded files contain malicious instructions	Treat document content as untrusted data, not executable instructions.
An agent executes unsafe operations	Restrict tools, validate arguments, and require approval for sensitive actions.
Model updates change teaching quality	Maintain a repeatable evaluation suite.
Database changes corrupt progress	Use transactions, backups, and schema migrations.
The app becomes too complicated	Build one complete learning journey before adding more subsystems.
One particularly important issue: prompt injection in learning materials
Your source materials might contain instructions that are irrelevant to the teaching task or deliberately crafted to manipulate an AI system.

A retrieved passage should not automatically become an instruction to the agent.

Keep the roles of source material and system instructions separate.

Limit the permissions of every tool and do not let document contents override your application policies.
